# Voice and language

## Hard rules

**No em-dashes. None.** This is the strongest single tell of machine-written prose and he has asked for them gone. Replace with:
- a comma, where the aside is light
- a colon, where the second half explains the first
- a full stop, where it's actually two sentences pretending to be one
- parentheses, sparingly, where it's genuinely parenthetical

Check the finished file for `—` and `--` before delivering. Zero occurrences.

**No "It's not X, it's Y" more than once in a piece.** Four of them reads as generated. One is fine and can be effective.

**No tricolons.** Three-item lists with parallel grammatical structure are a fingerprint. Break the parallelism or cut to two items.

**No perfectly balanced paragraphs.** Real writing has a two-sentence paragraph next to a nine-sentence one. If every paragraph is four to five sentences, the rhythm is wrong regardless of the content.

## Keep these

They are what make it read as human, and the instinct to polish them out is exactly the failure mode.

- **Hedges.** One or two "I'm not sure this generalises" moments per piece. Certainty on every claim reads as synthetic.
- **Digressions.** A short aside that doesn't serve the thesis is a signal of a person thinking.
- **Specific unglamorous details.** A system name, a wait time, a thing that went wrong, a number. Models are worst at faking these and readers weight them heavily.
- **The self-questioning turn.** Where he asks what he's actually good at, or whether he's been wrong. This is usually the best paragraph in any draft.
- **Verbatim tool exchanges.** Things like an agent replying "you're absolutely right, I should have checked that" prove hundreds of hours of real use in a way no assertion can.

## Trim these

- **Rhetorical "right?"** He uses it constantly when dictating. It works in speech and weakens sentences on a page. Cut most, keep one or two where the voice needs it.
- **"I mean" and "basically" as sentence openers.** Artefacts of dictation.
- **Stacked qualifiers.** "very very", "pretty much", "sort of" doubled up.
- **Vague plural nouns** where a specific one exists. "These sorts of tools" becomes the tool's name.

## Register

Direct, first person, technically confident without credentialing. He does not need to list his experience; the specifics do that work. If a sentence sounds like it's defending his seniority, cut it and replace with a concrete instance.

Concede generously and early. His arguments land harder when he has already given the other side its best case.

## Spelling

He writes mixed British and American forms. Pick whichever dominates his draft and make it consistent throughout, then tell him which one you standardised on so he can flip it.

## The final read

Before delivering, read the piece asking one question: could a competent stranger have written this from the same research?

If yes, something is missing. The fix is never more polish. It is putting a specific, unglamorous, first-hand detail back in.
