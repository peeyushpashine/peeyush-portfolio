There is a moment in most machine learning projects where a number comes back much better
than you expected, and you have about ten seconds to decide whether to celebrate or get
suspicious.

For a problem where two experienced practitioners routinely disagree, an area under the ROC
curve of 0.99 is not a good score. It is a confession. The question is what it is confessing
to, and the uncomfortable answer is usually that you have built a compression of your own
labelling rule and then graded it against that same rule.

This post is about how to catch that from the inside, which is harder than it sounds, because
the symptom is an absence rather than a presence. Two tests, both of which run in about a
minute on your laptop, and both of which I have made reproducible on public and synthetic data
so you can see them respond to a cause you control.

*A note on provenance. The concepts here are replicated from production work I cannot detail.
Everything numeric below is measured on public or synthetic data, generated by the scripts
linked at the end. Nothing in this post is a measurement of any system I have worked on.*

## The shape of the problem

Some problems arrive with labels. Most interesting ones do not.

When the thing you want to predict is a judgement rather than a fact, there is often no
corpus, no benchmark, and no internal set, because until your feature existed nobody had a
reason to record the judgement. Worse, the label may be genuinely subjective. Not
fuzzy-at-the-boundary subjective the way sentiment is. Properly subjective, where two
competent people give opposite answers on the same input within the same hour and both
explanations are good.

Barbara Plank's "The 'Problem' of Human Label Variation" (EMNLP 2022) makes the argument
better than I can: when annotators disagree systematically, the disagreement is information
about the task, not error to be averaged away. Chasing a single correct label is chasing
something that does not exist.

And yet your product has to output one label, because the interface has one column.

So you do the reasonable thing. You write down what the experts told you, encode it as a rule
cascade, run it over your history, and now you have labels. You train a model on them. It
scores 0.99.

![Rules make the labels, the labels train the model, the model is graded against the same labels](/diagrams/01-circularity-loop.svg)

Read that diagram and the problem is obvious. It is much less obvious from inside the work,
where each of those arrows was a sensible decision made weeks apart.

If your cascade is deterministic and non-conflicting, then given the same feature vector it
always produces the same label. The label function is, mathematically, a function. A learnable
one. A few hundred boosted trees will approximate it very accurately, because approximating a
deterministic function from its own outputs is one of the easiest tasks you can hand a
gradient-boosted ensemble.

**A model that reproduces its labelling function is not a model. It is a compression of the
labelling function.** It cannot know anything the rules did not already know. Every blind spot
in the cascade is reproduced faithfully, then hidden behind a respectable-looking score.

One aside worth making, because the vocabulary matters. This is not weak supervision. Weak
supervision in the Snorkel sense means multiple, conflicting, individually unreliable
labelling functions, plus a label model that learns each function's accuracy from the pattern
of agreement and disagreement. The conflict is the entire mechanism. A single non-conflicting
cascade is a different and much weaker thing, and calling it weak supervision lets you skip
the question of whether your labels are any good.

## Test one: the stump

The tell is not the score. Plenty of legitimate problems score 0.99.

The tell is that a deliberately weak model does almost as well as your real one.

Fit a decision tree capped at ten leaves alongside your actual model, and measure how much of
your model's lift over chance the stump recovers:

```
recovered = (stump_auc - 0.5) / (full_auc - 0.5)
```

If a ten-leaf tree recovers most of it, your label is a thin function of a few features, and
your large model is decoration.

Here is that diagnostic responding to a cause I control. Thirty independent features, three
thousand rows, and a label that depends on a growing number of them. Only the label's
structure changes:

```
 features in label   full AUC   stump AUC   lift recovered
                 1     1.0000      0.9997           99.9%
                 2     0.9993      0.9703           94.2%
                 3     0.9984      0.9688           94.1%
                 5     0.9898      0.8358           68.6%
                10     0.9808      0.8645           75.8%
                20     0.9635      0.7266           48.9%
                30     0.9515      0.7080           46.1%
```

Look at the first column. The full model scores between 0.95 and 1.00 the whole way down. If
you were reading only that number, every row here looks like a triumph. The stump column is
where the information is.

The same test on real data, using the thirty features of the Wisconsin breast cancer set with
two different label sources attached to them:

```
diagnostic label, assigned outside the feature set
  full 0.9907   stump 0.9164   lift recovered 84.9%

label written as a three-feature rule
  full 0.9991   stump 0.9885   lift recovered 97.9%
```

Same features. Same model. The only difference is where the label came from.

Treat anything above roughly ninety percent as a prompt to go and look, not as a verdict. Some
genuinely simple problems are genuinely simple.

## Test two: the gate trap

The stump test catches thin labels. This one catches something worse, and it is the finding I
would most want to hand to someone starting out.

**A labelling rule that references a feature the model also sees will produce circularity you
cannot detect from inside your own evaluation.**

Here is the shape, and it is so reasonable that it survives review every time. You have a
priority field. Your labelling rule is written safety-first: only assign the quiet label to
items at priority four or five. Do not teach the model that important things are ignorable.
Everyone nods. It ships.

Now follow the consequence. If a high-priority item can never receive the quiet label, then a
high-priority item that genuinely is routine noise, the health check somebody configured at
priority two three years ago and nobody has touched since, never appears in training as noise.
Not rarely. Never. The pattern is not hard to learn. It is **structurally unlearnable**. No
dataset would fix it, because the label generator cannot emit that label.

I built a synthetic world where I know the truth, so this can be watched rather than argued.
Twenty thousand items. Noise is a function of behaviour: how often the shape recurs, whether
it clears without a human, whether the source is flappy. Priority is only weakly correlated
with it, which is precisely why gating on priority feels safe.

```
High-priority items that are genuinely noise: 3,927 (19.6% of all items)
How many the labelling rule can ever mark as noise: 0
```

Train on the rule labels. Then grade the same model two ways.

```
GRADED AGAINST THE RULE LABELS (what your dashboard shows)
  AUC                                          1.0000
  high-priority noise cases in the test set    0   (none, so none can be failed)

GRADED AGAINST THE TRUTH (what is actually happening)
  AUC                                          0.6615
  recall on noise, low priority                0.4203
  recall on noise, HIGH priority               0.0000
```

A perfect score and a coin flip, from the same model on the same test set. The only thing that
changed is which answer key you grade against.

And notice the middle line of the first block. The evaluation does not report an error on
high-priority noise. It reports nothing, because there are no such cases in the exam. **The
absence of error is the symptom.** If your rules cannot see something, and your model
therefore cannot see it, and your evaluation is built from your rules, then your evaluation
will report zero error on the thing you are most wrong about.

One gate. Three failures. The model cannot learn a real pattern. The evaluation cannot detect
that it cannot learn it. And if you later want a statistical guarantee on your most dangerous
error type, you will find the calibration set structurally starved in exactly the region where
you needed it, because the labels that would have populated it were never generated.

---

## What to do instead

The fix, once named, is not dramatic.

**Move safety out of the label and into the decision layer.** Replace the hard gate with a
soft prior: allow the quiet label at any priority, require stronger evidence at high priority,
and keep the absolute "never auto-suppress a top-priority item" rule at inference time, where
it belongs. Safety in the decision layer protects users. Safety in the label corrupts the
data and then hides the corruption.

**Audit every labelling rule for features the model can also see.** This is a mechanical check
and it takes an afternoon. Any feature appearing in both places is a candidate for the trap.

**Enumerate what your cascade structurally cannot express, then look for those cases in your
evaluation set.** If you find none, that is not good news. That is the exam missing a section.

**Get one independent source of truth, however small.** A few hundred examples labelled by
people who never saw your rules will tell you more than another ten million rows from the same
generator. This is the expensive check and it is the only one that actually settles the
question.

## The reusable version

If you take one thing:

> When your labels come from a rule, your evaluation measures the rule's self-consistency.
> A high score is evidence about your rule, not about the world.

And two checks you can run this week, in ascending cost. Fit a ten-leaf tree next to your real
model and compare the lift. Then list every feature your labelling rules touch and check
whether the model sees it too.

Both scripts are on GitHub: [`stump_test.py`](https://github.com/peeyushpashine/peeyush-portfolio/blob/main/code/stump-test/stump_test.py) and [`gate_trap.py`](https://github.com/peeyushpashine/peeyush-portfolio/blob/main/code/stump-test/gate_trap.py). They need only
scikit-learn and they run in about a minute. If the diagnostic fires on something you own, I
would genuinely like to hear what you found.
